A Day in Miami, 2035: A Concept-Fiction Tour of the Future City

Before you read a word further: this is concept fiction (speculative fiction). Everything that happens in "a day in Miami, 2035" below is invented: the characters, the companies, the gadgets. We wrote it to imagine where today's real experiments could lead. To keep things honest, every invented technology comes with a Reality check separating what exists in 2026, verified and sourced, from what we made up.

So what might Miami look like in 2035? Wetter, hotter and more inventive. The real baseline is sobering: the Southeast Florida Regional Climate Compact projects 10 to 17 inches of sea-level rise by 2040 compared with 2000, and Miami-Dade now sees about 133 days a year above 90°F, up from roughly 85 in the 1960s, according to the county's Office of Resilience. Our fictional 2035 city answers with reefs that guard the shore, boats that drive themselves and fabric that fights the sun. Meet Lucía, an invented Little Havana-born art advisor, and follow her day.

6:12 a.m. Does the seawall really breathe?

Lucía jogs the Venetian Causeway at dawn. Below the railing, the seawall isn't gray concrete anymore. It's a lattice of textured "reef tiles" furred with sponges, oysters and baby corals, and threaded with sensors that pulse data to a county dashboard. At high tide the sensors note that the reef has grown 2 millimeters this month. Tourists call it the "breathing wall."

Reality check: Partly real. Miami startup Kind Designs installed its first living seawall panels on the Venetian Causeway in January 2025, with FIU researchers monitoring what grows on them, as Local 10 reported. The city-wide sensor network and growth dashboards are invented.

7:30 a.m. Who drives the water taxi?

Nobody. Lucía boards an electric, autonomous shuttle at Sunset Harbour. It glides across Biscayne Bay on a fixed lane, dodging paddleboarders with lidar, and drops her downtown in 18 minutes. The fare? Still free, a 2026 tradition the city never dropped.

Reality check: The route is real; the robot captain is invented. On January 20, 2026, the City of Miami Beach launched a free commuter water taxi between Sunset Harbour (Maurice Gibb Memorial Park) and Venetian Marina in Miami, using 40-foot boats carrying up to 55 people on a roughly 20-minute crossing. The boats have human captains.

In 2035, the most Miami thing you can do is commute by sea and arrive dry.

9:00 a.m. What is she wearing?

Her linen shirt goes pale when the sun hits 92°F and darkens when a cloud passes. The weave opens microscopic vents as her skin warms. On the street, half of Brickell is wearing the same "thermochromic linen," because the county's heat alerts now include a "fabric advisory."

Reality check: Invented. No such commercial heat-adaptive linen is sold in Miami today. What is real: Miami-Dade appointed the world's first county Chief Heat Officer in 2021 and released an Extreme Heat Action Plan in December 2022, focused on informing residents, cooling homes and cooling neighborhoods.

11:15 a.m. Can a mirror dress you?

In the Design District, Lucía steps in front of a boutique mirror. It reads the light, the humidity outside and her calendar ("client lunch, outdoor terrace"), then projects three looks onto her reflection, all from the store's stock and two from a vintage shop down the block. She buys the one the mirror flags as "lowest heat load."

Reality check: Invented. The AI stylist mirror is fiction. The Design District, of course, is real, and so is the appetite of Miami's luxury retail for anything that turns shopping into theater.

1:00 p.m. What's for lunch?

Stone crab claws, in July. Out of season? Not these. They're cultivated from cells in a lab in Doral, cracked tableside with the same mustard sauce your grandparents loved.

Reality check: Invented, and currently illegal. Florida's SB 1084, signed in May 2024, bans the manufacture and sale of cultivated meat in the state, and a federal appeals panel upheld the ban in March 2026. Stone crab season remains wild-caught and runs from October 15 to May 1.

3:30 p.m. Where does the seaweed go?

On Miami Beach, trucks rake brown sargassum off the sand and haul it to a plant near the port. There, in our story, microbes break it down into a resin that becomes beach-club cups, sunglasses frames and the plastic-free packaging on Lucía's afternoon cafecito.

Reality check: Mostly invented. There is no sargassum-to-bioplastic plant in Miami. Real precursors exist: Boston-based Carbonwave turns sargassum into biomaterials and has contracts to collect it near several Florida resorts, according to Waste360.

5:45 p.m. Can you fly from Brickell to the airport?

Lucía's client is landing at MIA. She takes an elevator to a Brickell rooftop vertiport, boards a four-seat electric air taxi and touches down in under ten minutes. The rooftop has a bar. Of course it does.

Reality check: Planned, not flying. In December 2025, Archer Aviation announced plans for a South Florida network linking MIA, FLL and PBI, with a vertiport at the Magic City Innovation District and Hard Rock Stadium's helipad, pending FAA certification of its Midnight aircraft. In March 2026 the FAA and DOT selected eight eVTOL pilot projects, including one led by the Florida Department of Transportation. The Brickell rooftop vertiport is our invention.

8:00 p.m. Is the art fair even here?

The night's event is a "holographic satellite" of an art fair. Paintings that physically hang in another city appear life-size in a warehouse in Allapattah, lit so precisely that collectors lean in to check the brushwork. A real gallerist, also projected, answers questions in Spanish, English and Portuguese.

Reality check: Invented. Volumetric holographic art fairs at this fidelity don't exist. The real fair calendar is spectacular enough: Miami Art Week fills the city every December.

10:30 p.m. How does she get home?

By train, partly. A friend arrives from Orlando on a quiet, packed evening train, and they share a car back to Little Havana.

Reality check: Real. Brightline runs intercity service between Miami and Orlando in roughly three hours and carried about 3.1 million passengers in 2025, its record. Its finances are shakier: in May 2026, WUSF reported that auditors flagged substantial doubt about its ability to continue as a going concern. Whether it's still running in 2035 is a real question, not fiction.

Through the lens

You can photograph 2035 today if you know where to look. Shoot the Venetian Causeway at low tide, when the living seawall panels are exposed: a macro lens shows the first barnacles and algae. Ride the free water taxi at 7 a.m. and frame downtown through the wake with a slow shutter for that "autonomous glide" look. In July, go to a heat-mapped street at noon and photograph shade itself: tree canopies, bus-stop shadows, people hugging walls. And for the future skyline, a long exposure from the MacArthur Causeway after dark turns every tail light into a flight path. Just remember to caption your images honestly: in this story, the reality checks matter as much as the dream.

Questions people ask

Is this article a prediction of Miami in 2035?

No. It's concept fiction: an invented, narrative day in 2035 meant to spark imagination. The technologies are made up or extrapolated. Each section includes a reality check that separates what is verified today, such as Brightline service or the Miami Beach water taxi, from what we invented for the story.

How much will sea level rise in Miami by 2040?

The Southeast Florida Regional Climate Compact's 2019 Unified Sea Level Rise Projection estimates 10 to 17 inches by 2040 and 21 to 54 inches by 2070, measured from 2000 mean sea level. In 2024 the Compact reviewed the projection and recommended its continued use for planning.

Will there be air taxis in Miami?

They're planned, not operating. Archer Aviation announced a South Florida network in December 2025 linking MIA, FLL and PBI, pending FAA certification of its aircraft. In March 2026 the FAA and DOT selected eight eVTOL pilot projects, including one led by the Florida Department of Transportation.

Can you buy lab-grown stone crab in Florida?

No. Florida banned the manufacture and sale of cultivated meat with SB 1084, signed in May 2024, and a federal appeals panel upheld the ban in March 2026. The lab-grown stone crab in this story is pure fiction and would require a change in state law.
