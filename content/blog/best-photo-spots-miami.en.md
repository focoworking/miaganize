It's 6:40 a.m. in Lummus Park and three tripods are already planted in the sand. Nobody talks. Everyone is aimed at the same mint-green lifeguard tower, waiting for the exact second the sun breaks over the Atlantic. In Miami, light is currency, and the people who know get up early to collect it.

The direct answer: the best places to photograph in Miami are South Pointe Park and the South Beach lifeguard towers at sunrise, Ocean Drive and the MiMo motels of Biscayne Boulevard at night, Wynwood and the Design District in the morning, Vizcaya and the Cape Florida Lighthouse for architecture and history, and the Brickell skyline at blue hour from Virginia Key. You don't need a permit for personal photos. For commercial work in public space, you do, and every city has its own process.

## Miami Beach: sunrise or nothing

South Beach faces east, and that decides everything. **South Pointe Park** gives you the sun rising nearly in line with the pier, and in the afternoon, cruise ships leaving the port through Government Cut just yards from shore: human scale against a floating building is a shot that never misses.

A few blocks north, the **lifeguard towers** of Lummus Park are the city's most repeated portrait, for good reason. Many were redesigned by architect William Lane after Hurricane Andrew, each in its own palette. Arrive 20 minutes before sunrise: the pre-dawn pinks and lavenders flatter the paint more than direct sun does.

Shoot **Ocean Drive** twice. At sunrise, with no tables or cars, the Art Deco façades look like their original drawings. At night, the neon does the work. And if you're here in January, Art Deco Weekend (January 15–17, 2027) fills the street with classic cars.

> In Miami, light is currency, and the people who know get up early to collect it.

## Wynwood and the Design District: art in the backlight

**Wynwood Walls** is a ticketed open-air museum. Per its [FAQ](https://web.thewynwoodwalls.com/faqs), personal photography and video are welcome as long as the content isn't monetized; for a commercial shoot, email them first. Outside the compound, the street murals are on public roads: free for your Instagram, but a commercial production needs a City of Miami permit.

In the **Design District**, Buckminster Fuller's Fly's Eye Dome and the five façades of the Museum Garage photograph best early, before the boutiques open. It's a privately managed district: for shoots with models or brands, get approval from management.

## Downtown and Brickell: a skyline that changes every month

Herzog & de Meuron's **PAMM** and the bayfront park beside it offer columns, shadows and hanging gardens, with the port as a backdrop. The **Freedom Tower** (1925), modeled on Seville's Giralda and known as the "Ellis Island of the South" for its role with Cuban exiles, wants morning light on its façade.

For the full skyline, cross the **Rickenbacker Causeway** to Virginia Key. From there the sun sets behind Brickell: sunset gives you silhouettes, blue hour gives you detail. Shoot the Waldorf Astoria under construction now; in a couple of years it will be a different photo.

!! 1,049 feet: the planned height of the Waldorf Astoria, Miami's first supertall, now rising in Downtown (Dezeen, 2025).

## Vizcaya, Cape Florida and Coral Gables: historic Miami

**Vizcaya** is the favorite backdrop for quinceañeras, weddings and proposals, which is why it has clear rules. Per its [official page](https://vizcaya.org/visit-2/photography-and-filming/), you need a photography permit when your visit meets two of several conditions (shoot purpose, a designated photographer, equipment such as tripods), and staff can deny entry without one. Portrait permits are personal, capped daily, and can't be used commercially. Commercial filming is another world: the museum lists permits starting at $20,000, outdoors only, in the gardens and on the terraces.

!! $20,000: the starting price of a commercial filming permit at Vizcaya (Vizcaya Museum & Gardens).

The **Cape Florida Lighthouse**, in Bill Baggs Cape Florida State Park, was completed in 1825 and rebuilt in 1846; it's the oldest standing structure in the county, according to [Florida State Parks](https://floridastateparks.org/parks-and-trails/bill-baggs-cape-florida-state-park/history). The park opens at 8 a.m. and closes at sunset, and shuts its gates when it reaches capacity on weekends, so go early.

!! 1846: the year the current Cape Florida Lighthouse tower was completed, Miami-Dade's oldest standing structure (Florida State Parks).

Coral Gables' **Venetian Pool**, opened in 1924 in a former coral rock quarry, glows at midday, when the water turns turquoise. Check the City of Coral Gables' rules before bringing professional gear.

## What permits do I need to shoot in Miami?

Miami isn't one jurisdiction, and that trips everyone up. The general rule: **personal photos in public space, free; commercial production on public property, permit required.**

- **City of Miami** (Downtown, Brickell, Wynwood, Little Havana, Coconut Grove): the [Film Office](https://www.miami.gov/Permits-Construction/Filming-and-Events) asks for permit applications at least 3 business days before the shoot. Questions: filmoffice@miamigov.com.
- **Miami Beach**: the Film & Print permit is free, but mandatory for commercial photo and video projects on public property and at residential locations, per the city's [guidelines](https://www.miamibeachfl.gov/city-hall/tourism-culture/film-print/guidelines/). It requires a certificate of insurance with at least $1 million in coverage. Crews larger than 12 need an extended permit with an off-duty officer. Shoots inside private commercial property (hotels, restaurants) generally don't need a city permit, but they do need the owner's.
- **Miami-Dade County** (county parks, Virginia Key, public transit): the [Office of Film and Entertainment](https://www.filmiami.org/permit_who.asp) issues Film/Photo permits for county property, and transit has its own rules.
- **Private property** (Wynwood Walls, Design District, hotels, Vizcaya): the owner decides. No approval, no commercial shoot.

## Drones: Miami's sky isn't open

Much of the urban area sits inside Miami International Airport's controlled airspace. What the [FAA](https://www.faa.gov/uas/recreational_flyers) asks of recreational pilots: pass the free TRUST test and carry the certificate, register any drone over 250 grams (0.55 lb), stay below 400 feet, and get LAANC authorization to fly in controlled airspace. No flying inside temporary flight restrictions (TFRs), which tend to pop up around big events; check before takeoff.

In **Miami Beach**, any production flight needs a [Drone Fly Permit](https://www.miamibeachfl.gov/city-hall/tourism-culture/film-print/types-of-permits/drone-permits/): file at least 5 business days ahead, with a remote pilot certificate (Part 107), FAA registration, a flight plan with altitude and route, and insurance.

## Through the lens

- **Plan by light, not by map:** beaches at sunrise, Wynwood and the Design District in the morning, the skyline at blue hour, MiMo and Ocean Drive at night.
- **Lenses:** a 24–70 covers almost everything; a wide angle for the Fly's Eye Dome and interiors; a 70–200 to compress the skyline from Virginia Key.
- **Humidity:** step out of an air-conditioned car and your lens fogs. Take the camera out 10 minutes early.
- **People:** on Calle Ocho and at Domino Park, ask before you shoot a portrait. The best photo in Little Havana usually starts with a conversation.
- **Paperwork:** if you're getting paid for the shoot, assume you need a permit. In Miami Beach it's free, and it saves you a ruined morning.
