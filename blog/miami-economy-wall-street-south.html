Wall Street South: How Miami's $260 Billion Economy Really Works

A $2.5 billion Citadel tower, 28 million visitors and 8.5 million cruise passengers. Miami's economy is booming, and so is its affordability problem.

By MIAGAZINE Editorial

At 7 a.m. on Brickell Bay Drive, three Miamis share one block. Behind construction fencing, excavators work the future site of Citadel's global headquarters. A few steps away, a hotel valet unloads luggage from a Brazilian family's SUV. Across the bay, two cruise ships sit at PortMiami, ready to send thousands of passengers to the Caribbean by sunset.

Short answer: Miami's economy in 2026 is bigger, richer and more diverse than ever, and also harder to live in. Miami-Dade produced about $260.8 billion in GDP in 2024. Finance has moved in, with Citadel as the flagship. But tourism, trade and real estate still carry much of the weight, and the region's biggest open question is whether the people who staff that economy can still afford to live in it.

How big is Miami's economy, really?

Big enough to compare with countries. According to Bureau of Economic Analysis county data published via FRED, Miami-Dade's GDP reached about $260.8 billion in current dollars in 2024, up from roughly $244.8 billion in 2023. Adjusted for inflation, USAFacts puts 2024 growth at 3.4%.

$260.8 billionMiami-Dade County GDP in 2024, current dollars (BEA via FRED)

The breakdown by industry is the revealing part. The county's largest industry by output is real estate and rental and leasing, at about $37.7 billion in real terms in 2024, followed by professional and business services at about $31.1 billion (USAFacts, BEA data). In other words, Miami's economy rests heavily on property, and finance is growing on top of that base.

Why do they call it "Wall Street South"?

Because the money moved in. Since 2020, hedge funds, trading firms and investment managers have opened or moved offices to South Florida, drawn by no state income tax, the time zone, the weather and an aggressive recruiting pitch from local leaders. Elliott Management moved its headquarters to West Palm Beach, and many firms set up offices along Brickell Avenue.

The flagship is Citadel. Ken Griffin moved the firm's headquarters from Chicago to Miami in 2022, and in 2026 the project at 1201 Brickell Bay Drive got bigger. The Real Deal reported in April 2026 that Griffin had expanded the office component and dropped a planned hotel. Press reports now put the Foster + Partners–designed, 54-story tower at about $2.5 billion, up from an original estimate around $1 billion, with a residential building and a large garage added to the plan. Completion is expected in the early 2030s.

$2.5 billionthe current estimated cost of Citadel's 54-story Brickell headquarters, up from about $1 billion originally (press reports, 2026)

The effect goes beyond one building. Finance jobs pay high salaries, and those salaries feed restaurants, galleries, private schools, yacht brokers and condo sales. In the Miami–Miami Beach–Kendall metro division, professional and business services employment grew 2.4% year over year as of March 2026, among the fastest rates in the state for that sector, according to CareerSource South Florida's monthly release.

Wall Street South didn't replace Miami's old economy. It moved in on top of it.

Is tourism still the engine?

Absolutely. The Greater Miami Convention & Visitors Bureau's State of the Industry report (May 2025) counted more than 28 million visitors in 2024, an all-time record. About 20.1 million stayed overnight and 8.1 million came as day-trippers. Together they spent about $22 billion and generated roughly $2.2 billion in state and local taxes.

28 million+visitors to Greater Miami & Miami Beach in 2024, a record (GMCVB)

That's the base the luxury economy relies on: hotels, restaurants, Art Week, Formula 1, boat shows, and 2026 World Cup matches at Hard Rock Stadium. Each event brings in high-spending visitors, and some of them come back to buy a condo.

What does PortMiami add?

Scale, and a very visible symbol. In fiscal year 2025 (October 2024 to September 2025), PortMiami handled a record 8,564,225 cruise passengers, up about 4% from 8,233,056 the year before, according to Cruise Industry News. Port Canaveral edged past it with about 8.6 million, so the "Cruise Capital of the World" title is now contested.

Cruise passengers only tell part of the story. The port is also a cargo gateway to Latin America and the Caribbean, which supports the trade, logistics and banking ties that made Miami a regional capital long before hedge funds arrived.

Can Miami's workers afford Miami?

This is where the boom shows its costs. The job market is tight: Miami-Dade's unemployment rate was 2.5% in July 2026, 2.1 points below Florida's 4.6%, according to CareerSource South Florida. Almost everyone who wants a job has one.

The trouble is housing. MIAMI REALTORS data put the Miami-Dade single-family median at about $695,000 in June 2026. Condos are cheaper, but condo owners now face higher association dues and special assessments under Florida's post-Surfside safety laws. Insurance has improved but is still expensive. Meanwhile, MIAMI REALTORS reports that domestic migration into Miami-Dade keeps rising, with New York as the top source, and those movers often arrive with northeastern salaries.

The result is a familiar boomtown pattern:

  • Upward pressure: high-income newcomers and foreign buyers (who spent $4.4 billion on South Florida homes in 2025, per the MIAMI REALTORS International Report) push prices up.
  • Workforce strain: hospitality, healthcare and service workers, who keep the tourism economy running, commute longer distances or leave the county.
  • Policy response: workforce-housing programs, zoning changes and incentives exist, but they are moving more slowly than prices.

National outlets have started describing South Florida's shrinking middle class as the downside of the wealth boom. For Miami, it is the issue that will decide whether the boom lasts.

What does it mean for luxury and the creative economy?

For luxury brands, Wall Street South means more permanent clients, not just seasonal ones. Fund managers who live here year-round buy art, eat at tasting-menu restaurants on weeknights, enroll children in private schools and furnish branded condos. That year-round demand supports luxury retail in places like the Design District and Bal Harbour, and helps explain why branded residences keep launching.

For the creative economy, the picture is mixed. More money means more collectors, more commissions, more galleries and more photo shoots. But studio space in Wynwood and Little River costs more each year, and the artists, stylists and photographers who give Miami its image are among the first priced out. The city's appeal depends on that creative class, so keeping them here is an economic issue, not only a cultural one.

Through the lens

For photographers: Shoot the Citadel site now from the Brickell Bay Drive sidewalk; in a few years the hole in the ground will be a skyline landmark, and the before-and-after series will be worth having. For a full economic portrait in one frame, go to the MacArthur Causeway at sunrise: cruise ships at PortMiami, cargo cranes, downtown towers and the bay all fit in a long lens. On a weekday at 8 a.m., the Brickell Metromover station gives you the finance crowd in motion.

For readers: Ride the free Metromover loop from Brickell to Omni to see the whole downtown economy in one ride. Have lunch at a Brickell restaurant to hear the mix of English, Spanish and Portuguese deal talk. Then go to Little River or Allapattah, where Miami's creative economy is still affordable enough to make things. The economy is visible from the street if you know where to look.

Questions people ask

Why is Miami called Wall Street South?

Because hedge funds, trading firms and investment managers have moved headquarters or major offices to South Florida since 2020. The flagship case is Citadel, which moved its headquarters from Chicago to Miami and is building a 54-story tower on Brickell Bay Drive, now projected at about $2.5 billion.

How big is Miami-Dade's economy?

Miami-Dade County's GDP was about $260.8 billion in current dollars in 2024, up from roughly $244.8 billion in 2023, according to BEA county data published via FRED. In real terms it grew 3.4%. Real estate and rental is the largest industry, followed by professional and business services.

How many tourists visit Miami each year?

The Greater Miami Convention & Visitors Bureau reported more than 28 million visitors in 2024, an all-time record: about 20.1 million overnight visitors and 8.1 million day-trippers. They spent about $22 billion and generated roughly $2.2 billion in state and local taxes.

Is Miami affordable to live in?

Increasingly less so for local workers. Single-family homes in Miami-Dade had a median price around $695,000 in June 2026, according to MIAMI REALTORS, and insurance and condo fees add pressure. Unemployment is very low, but wages for many service jobs haven't kept up with housing costs.
